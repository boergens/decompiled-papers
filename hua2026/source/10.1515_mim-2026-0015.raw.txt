Preparing large biological specimens
for high-throughput volume electron microscopy
https://doi.org/10.1515/mim-2026-0015
Received July 30, 2026; accepted August 31, 2026;
published online September 24, 2026

Abstract: To enable high-throughput volume electron
microscopy (vEM) imaging, osmium-based staining
methods are routinely employed to enhance membrane
contrast and electrical conductivity in biological specimens.
Nevertheless, reliable preparation of densely and uniformly
stained tissue blocks remains a major challenge for
large-scale vEM-based ultrastructure analysis, especially
for connectomic studies. Recently, multiple strategies have
been proposed to achieve high-quality en bloc staining
of millimeter-sized brain samples without compromising
membrane contrast, paving the way for system-level
neural circuit mapping using vEM. Herein, we review the
development of large-volume tissue staining and elaborate
the underlying chemical logic based on cumulative
experimental evidence. We further discuss emerging
challenges associated with adapting current staining
strategies for cryo-preserved tissue samples and in-resin
applications of correlative light and electron microscopy.
Keywords: volume electron microscopy; large-scale connectomics; osmium staining; en bloc tissue processing;
high-throughput imaging
Over the past two decades, conventional serial ultra-thin
section electron microscopy has evolved into a group
of emerging techniques that enable automated acquisition of large-scale volumetric image datasets from samples thicker than one micron, collectively termed volume

electron microscopy (vEM) [1]. Based on imaging principles, vEM can be classiﬁed into two major categories: serial
section-based and serial block-face-based methods [2]–[5].
Serial section-based vEM implements automated collection of ultrathin sections either on opaque tape for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [6], or on grid tape for
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [7] and electron
tomography (ET) [8]. Since tape-related artifacts complicate downstream data analysis, direct silicon wafer-based
section collection techniques have recently been developed
with the assistance of magnetic ﬁeld-based systems (MagC
[9], GAUSS-EM [10]) or an ionizer-equipped device (DriftEM [11]). These methods are non-destructive and therefore allow post-staining of mounted sections, but additional
sample processing inevitably causes section loss, folding,
contamination, especially for collecting large datasets. In
contrast, serial block-face vEM produce intrinsically aligned
image stacks by iteratively imaging newly exposed sample
surfaces through in-chamber ultramicrotomy cutting [12]
or focused ion beam milling [13]. While this workﬂow circumvents the major drawbacks of traditional ultra-thin sectioning, it mandates the use of en bloc-stained tissue blocks
due to an integrated imaging and surface removal process.
This scenario also applies to large-scale ion beam etching of
thick sections [14], [15], which is regarded as a parallelized
block-face imaging modality. Furthermore, sufficient heavymetal impregnation is indispensable for mitigating charging
artifact under SEM, especially for imaging large biological
samples or conducting fast imaging under high electron
dose. As supplementary anti-charging strategies, conductive
resin embedding [16], carbon nanotube-coated ATUM tape
[17], and in-chamber focal charge compensation [18] are
often required to image speciﬁc tissue samples prone to
charging.

within a superﬁcial layer of approximately 200 μm from
the tissue surface. Note that these stained samples exhibited low-contrast central regions encapsulated by a dense
layer of black precipitates (Figure 1B). Such staining inhomogeneity could not be simply attributed to slow reagent
penetration and persisted even after prolonged incubation,
representing a substantial bottleneck for en bloc staining
of large-volume tissues. This notion was later supported by
real-time monitoring of the stain-penetration process using
X-ray microscopy [28]. In addition, a 10–15 % sample shrinkage in aldehyde-ﬁxed brain tissues was observed after treatment with osmium and ferrocyanide mixtures, offering one
plausible explanation for the observed diffusion-barrier formation [28].

As the practical utility of serial block-face vEM is constrained by the maximum sample size processable using
rOTO staining, early methodological advances in this ﬁeld
were primarily pioneered by Winfried Denk and his followers (Table 1). In 2015, three independent research teams
proposed distinct strategies to resolve the bottleneck of
large-volume sample preparation. Collectively, these studies
demonstrated that the mixture of OsO4 and ferrocyanide
(“reduced osmium”) is responsible for the undesirable precipitation barrier that forms within brain tissue samples
[26], [27]. As a straightforward solution, Hua et al. developed
a modiﬁed procedure featuring sequential OsO4 penetration and ferrocyanide reduction to replace the conventional
reduced-osmium step in the classic rOTO protocol [26]. Note
that an intermediate wash step between OsO4 and ferrocyanide incubation is considered unnecessary, as reducedosmium species will be generated to some extent. This simple modiﬁcation, termed the ORTO protocol (also informally
referred to as Hua protocol), enables uniform staining of
biopsy samples up to 1 mm in diameter (Figure 1C). In parallel, Mikula and Denk established the BROPA (brain-wide
reduced-osmium staining with pyrogallol-mediated ampliﬁcation, or rOPO) protocol [27]. This procedure incorporates
10 % formamide to stabilize reduced-osmium species and

Since then, en bloc staining using these rOTO variants has become popular within the vEM community [14],
[37]–[41], including serial-section vEM laboratories, owing
to its operational simplicity (eliminating the need for poststaining of sections) and enhanced imaging throughput [7],
[42]–[46]. More importantly, different concepts established
in these studies provide a foundational framework for
developing a common procedure scalable to diverse tissue
dimensions (Figure 2). For instance, sequential OsO4 penetration and ferrocyanide reduction procedure were prolonged and performed at 4 ◦ C to prepare the whole insect
brains [30], [47]. More recently, the modiﬁed ORTO protocol
has been combined with ECS preservation to enable staining
of human and rodent surgical brain samples up to 37 mm3 in
volume, with one dimension below 2 mm [32]. Alternatively,
it has also incorporated the TCH-to-pyrogallol substitution
(from BROPA) in a prolonged, low-temperature implementation adapted for whole mouse brains and human brain
specimens [31].
In addition to compounds of osmium, other heavy metals are adopted to further increase the overall electron contrast of biological structures. A two-step uranyl acetate incubation procedure has been established and implemented
after osmium staining of brain tissues [26]. Prolonged incubation (overnight to 2 days) at 4 ◦ C enables long-distance
stain diffusion without inducing cytoplasmic darkening,
while a short warming step (2–5 h) at room temperature
or 50 ◦ C enhances the reactivity of uranyl acetate with protein and membrane targets [26], [31], [32]. For oversized
samples, note that the application of a high-concentration
(4 %) staining solution is recommended [31]; alternatively,
repeated replacement with fresh solution during incubation
can ensure sufficient stain supply. Regarding another widely
used staining buffer, the Walton solution (lead aspartate),
early efforts have attempted to attenuate stain deposition
by lowering the pH of the staining buffer from 5.5 to 5 [26].
However, lead aspartate solution is found unsuitable for
long-term incubation due to its high susceptibility to light
and air exposure. Consequently, it is not surprisingly that

2 Putative chemical mechanisms
of reduced osmium staining
Despite the widespread application of these reducedosmium-based protocols for preparing various biological specimens, the detailed chemical reactions underlying
membrane contrast generation remains incompletely elucidated. A prevailing model describing reduced-osmium
staining was initially proposed by Hua et al. [26] and
has recently been revisited through chemical analysis
[51]. The model can be simpliﬁed (Figure 3) and used
to rationalize the enhanced membrane-speciﬁc osmium
deposition achieved by supplementing ferrocyanide, which
in consensus functions as a reducing agent [20], [26],
[51]. First, OsO4 exists in both nonpolar (lipophilic) and

Second, OsO4 undergoes a multi-step reduction process that
yields water-insoluble OsO2 . This black solid preferentially
deposits on membrane surface, acting as the core substance
responsible for generating membrane contrast under electron microscopy [26], [51]. Third, ferrocyanide may directly
reduce ionized OsO4 to intermediate OsVI species outside
lipid bilayers, prompting further reduction to OsO2 . Effectively, the addition of ferrocyanide to the OsO4 solution
establishes a shortcut for OsO2 generation, thereby maximizing membrane contrasting. However, replacing ferrocyanide with other reducing agents such as Na2 SO3 /NaHSO3
has failed to yield comparable results, implying that ferrocyanide may exert additional roles beyond osmium reduction. Moreover, these proposed reactions are strongly pHdependent (Figure 3), which warrants further investigations
to identify the optimal working conditions for osmium deposition in a controlled fashion.
By and large, the proposed reaction model is consistent
with experimental observations obtained across a range of
osmium staining methods [26]. As for the original reduced
osmium recipe, ferrocyanide rapidly depletes available
OsO4 to generate water-soluble OsIV species. Although this
process leads to more OsO2 deposition onto cellular structures, the resultant low OsO4 concentration restricts the
tissue penetration depth of staining. More importantly,

this mechanism provides a plausible explanation for why
sequential incubation with OsO4 and ferrocyanide allows
OsO4 to diffuse across greater distances before being converted into tissue-immobile OsO2 deposits (Figure 4). Under
certain circumstances, however, the water-soluble intermediate osmium products (OsVI ) may remain stabilized
for an extended duration and may be washed out of the
tissue prior to forming OsO2 , the ﬁnal reduction product. Until recently, the model seems unable to explain
the progressive stain washout observed during prolonged
ferrocyanide incubation (>3 h) [28], [31]. To resolve this
discrepancy, it has been suggested that extended ferrocyanide incubation removes OsVI species in the extracellular
matrix through forming stable coordination compounds,
which reduces background signals and thereby enhancing membrane contrast [31]. Whether this pathway represents the primary mechanism of ferrocyanide-enhanced
osmium staining remains controversial, considering that
OsO4 -ferrocyanide mixtures do effectively stain various tissues within a short period and yield greater heavy-metal
impregnation than OsO4 treatment alone [20], [23]. Building on recent advances in low-temperature staining of
insect [30], [47] and mammalian brain samples [31], we
speculate that slow extraction of membrane lipids and
biomolecules owing to osmium overﬁxation may largely
account for osmium washout during prolonged incubation
steps (Figure 4). This hypothesis is supported by cumulative evidence as well as our own experience showing that

3 Future improvement of vEM
sample preparation
Although the preparation of large-volume tissue samples
is technically feasible via prolonged low-temperature incubation, further protocol optimization with shortened processing durations and incorporate quality-control checkpoints in between critical steps is desirable to improve
overall staining success rates. Since both stain-diffusion and
chemical-reaction rates decline at low temperatures, incubations required for large specimens must be substantially
prolonged. If overﬁxation-induced osmium washout cannot be fully suppressed even under low-temperature conditions, one would expect that excessively prolonged incubation may lead to noticeable destaining before thorough

stain penetration is accomplished in oversized samples.
Further optimization of chemical ﬁxation is therefore
required to minimize membrane lipid extraction, for
example by microwave-assisted osmium ﬁxation strategies
[52].
Another unresolved challenge in vEM sample preparation lies in tissue ﬁxation artifacts introduced by conventional chemical crosslinking of biomolecules. Previous studies have documented prominent morphological alternations in chemically ﬁxed neural tissues, including reduced
extracellular spaces [29], distorted neurite and synapse
morphology [53], [54], and displaced synaptic vesicles [55].
The extent to which these ﬁxation-induced morphological changes would inﬂuence biophysical modeling and
connectome-based emulation remains to be clariﬁed. As a
promising approach, high-pressure-freezing (HPF) enables
near-native ultrastructural preservation for tissue specimens up to 200 μm thick by suppressing ice crystal formation through rapid vitriﬁcation under high pressure.
In principle, water molecules within HPF-preserved tissues can be gradually substituted with acetone under cryocondition (freeze substitution), allowing subsequent resin
embedding at room temperature. Afterwards, these samples
can be handled normally for both serial section-based and
serial block-face-based vEM (Figure 6), except for lower

to implement during or after freeze substitution. To fully
unlock the potential of HPF-preserved tissues for largescale vEM applications, future methodological advances are
needed to establish effective en bloc staining strategies compatible with the freeze-substitution workﬂow. Promising
approaches include the use of an acetone-based cocktail
solution containing OsO4 , uranyl acetate, tannic acid, and
potassium permanganate [56]. Similarly, pyrogallol and gallic acid are expected to serve as potential osmiophilic candidates for stain ampliﬁcation [27], [57], given their high
solubility in acetone. Alternatively, supplementing freezesubstitution solutions with 5 % water has been proposed
to enable the use of water-soluble stains for cellular ultrastructure and membrane, such as a mixture of permanganate and uranium salts, as a cost-effective replacement
for OsO4 [58]. More recently, complete sample rehydration
is carried out after freeze substitution using an acetone
solution containing 2 % OsO4 and 3 % water. This workﬂow,
termed FSaqOTO [59], supports the subsequent aqueous
osmium ampliﬁcation procedure as well as post-staining
with uranium and lead solutions. Furthermore, emerging
image processing tools [60], [61], originally developed to
boost vEM imaging throughput, can be repurposed to compensate for the reduced image contrast typically observed
in HPF-preserved samples (Supplementary Video 1).
Last but not least, multiplexed labeling and correlative imaging with light microscopy (LM) represent

important trends in modern vEM workﬂows [62]–[65].
Recent advances have enabled cell- and organelle-speciﬁc
labeling using APEX-based probes [66], [67] as well as
ﬁxation-resistant and chemically stable ﬂuorescent proteins
[68]–[70], allowing for identiﬁcation of genetically targeted
cellular structures under EM. To date, however, these strategies have only been validated in samples processed with
a single round of osmiﬁcation [69] or with uranyl acetate
to replace strong oxidative osmium compounds [64]. Further optimization of stain deposition density is therefore
required to achieve unambiguous discrimination between
labeled and unlabeled cyto-structures, while maximally preserving ﬂuorescence after enhanced osmiﬁcation and resin
embedding for in situ correlative LM and vEM imaging.

4 Summary
Optimized osmium-based en bloc staining has made largevolume and even whole-brain mapping technically achievable via commercial high-throughput vEM. With the growing advancement of high-speed imaging techniques and
artiﬁcial intelligence-empowered acquisition optimization,
saturated membrane staining is anticipated to no longer
be the primary bottleneck for vEM experiments. Instead,
increasing research attention will shift toward other exciting directions: developing accelerated or simpliﬁed workﬂows that can accomplish sample preparation within hours
to days, enhancing contrast for HPF-preserved samples
for large-scale high-throughput vEM, and improving the
