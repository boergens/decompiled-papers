Figure 2: Protocol development for high-contrast en bloc staining. Joint efforts have greatly promoted the development of en bloc staining techniques
for specimens ranging from small brain tissues to whole brains. To the best of our knowledge, Deerinck et al. first implemented rOTO staining
for brain samples, laying a foundational framework for subsequent protocol optimization. Hua et al. further proposed a separate OsO4 penetration
and reduction routine (OR, magenta) to achieve homogenous staining of 1-mm samples. Meanwhile, Mikula et al. established a pyrogallol-mediated
osmium amplification (PO) strategy and validated that the preservation of extracellular space (ESC, blue), a method originally developed by Palloto
et al., can substantially facilitate stain penetration into brain tissues. Notably, 10 % formamide is supplemented to the reduced osmium mixture (rO)
to enhance solution stability during prolonged incubation. Alternatively, Polilov et al. performed a prolonged OR treatment at low temperature (4 ◦ C,
yellow) for insect brains. More recently, Song et al. integrated prolonged OR treatment with PO amplification at 4 ◦ C, enabling uniform staining of the
entire mouse brain. In parallel, Karlupia et al. optimized room-temperature ORTO staining for surgical samples with preserved ECS. Collectively,
the newly established low-temperature ORPO workflow exhibits great potential for further improving the overall efficiency and quality of en bloc brain
staining.

      Although these protocols were originally developed
for EM imaging, they have gained increasing recogni-
tion as ready-to-use sample preparation approaches for
X-ray tomography [48], [49] and photoemission electron
microscopy [50]. Moreover, osmium-based staining is lipid-
selective and in principle readily applicable to other organ
types. However, the incubation durations for key steps must
still be determined empirically, due to heterogeneous tis-
sue densities. This process can be both costly and time-
consuming, as it requires repeated test runs with in-situ
monitoring via X-ray microscopy and ﬁnal validation of
sample quality using electron microscopy [28], [31].
